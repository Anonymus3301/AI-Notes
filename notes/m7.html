7.1Stated problem vs actual problem

In one line: clients describe a solution (“we need a chatbot”); your job is to find the underlying problem (“agents spend 40% of their time searching three wikis”) and the metric that proves you fixed it.

Analogy. A patient says “I need antibiotics”. A good doctor asks where it hurts, since when, and what they have tried.

Core ideas

Ask about the past (what actually happened), not the future (what they would do). Past behaviour is evidence; hypotheticals are politeness. Then drill down with “why?” until you reach a cause you can measure.

Discovery question bank.

  • “Walk me through the last time this went wrong.” (a story, not an opinion)
  • “What happens today, step by step, and who touches it?”
  • “What does it cost when it fails: time, money, risk?”
  • “If this worked perfectly, what number changes, and who notices?”
  • “What have you tried before, and why did it stop?”
  • “Who has to say yes for this to go live?”
flowchart TD
  ASK["Stated: build a chatbot"] --> WHY1["Why? Support is slow"]
  WHY1 --> WHY2["Why? Agents search 3 wikis per ticket"]
  WHY2 --> WHY3["Why? Policies are scattered and outdated"]
  WHY3 --> REAL["Actual: findable, current answers for agents"]
  REAL --> MET["Metric: median handle time down 25 percent"]
Problem statement template
For  [user group]
who  [struggle with X, measured as Y today]
we will [capability], so that [metric] moves from [baseline] to [target] by [date].
Out of scope: [list]

Trap. Asking “would you use a feature that…?” Everyone says yes to hypotheticals. Ask about past behaviour instead.

Read: The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick) · Five whys (Wikipedia) · Jobs to be done (Christensen Institute)

7.2Scoping under political and budget constraints

In one line: pick the smallest slice that proves value to the person who controls the budget, avoids the political landmines, and can ship in weeks, not quarters.

Analogy. Building a bridge across a river with angry landowners on both banks. Start with a footbridge where everyone agrees, prove people use it, then widen it.

7.3Engagement artefacts — PRD-lite, SOW, ADRs

In one line: a one-page PRD aligns what and why, the statement of work fixes what you deliver and when, and architecture decision records capture why you chose X over Y.

Analogy. The PRD is the trip plan, the SOW is the booking confirmation, ADRs are the diary entries explaining why you took the mountain road.

# PRD-lite: Agent Policy Assistant
Problem: Agents take 6 min median to find policy answers.
Users: 120 tier-1 support agents (Hindi + English).
Goal: Median lookup time < 90 s; answer accuracy ≥ 90% on golden set.
Scope: Refund, KYC, fraud policies (≈400 pages). Web UI inside the CRM.
Out of scope: Customer-facing bot, auto-replies, voice.
Risks: Outdated policies; PII in tickets; SSO integration lead time.
Success check: Week 6 pilot with 20 agents, A/B against current process.

# ADR-003: Use hybrid search (BM25 + vectors) with reranking
Status: Accepted — 2026-11-01
Context: Agents search by policy codes (e.g. "RF-12") and by natural language.
Decision: Hybrid retrieval with RRF, cross-encoder rerank top 30 → 5.
Alternatives: Pure vector (missed codes: hit@5 0.71); BM25 only (missed paraphrases: 0.64).
Consequences: +120 ms p95 latency; one more model to host; hit@5 0.89.

SOW essentials: deliverables, milestones and dates, acceptance criteria, assumptions (for example “client provides data access by week 1”), an out-of-scope list, a change process, and fees.

Reading the examples. The PRD has numbers (90 s, 90%) so success is testable. The ADR records the rejected alternatives with evidence, which stops the same debate being reopened six months later.

Trap. A SOW without assumptions. If the data arrives three weeks late, the assumptions clause is what protects your timeline.

Read: ADR GitHub organisation · Michael Nygard: Documenting architecture decisions · Atlassian: product requirements

7.4Getting data out — firewalls, read replicas, change data capture

In one line: never hammer the client’s production database; read from a replica or export, and use change data capture (CDC) to stream inserts and updates as events.

Analogy. Instead of repeatedly asking the busy shopkeeper “anything new?”, you install a doorbell that rings every time stock changes (CDC).

    if not event:

    op, after = event["op"], event.get("after")      # c=create, u=update, d=delete

Reading the code. The connector reads the database’s write-ahead log (from the replica, so production is untouched), and the password comes from a file reference, not the config. The consumer handles all three operations; handling d is what keeps deleted policies out of your answers.

Trap. Asking for broad database credentials. Request a least-privilege read-only user on specific tables, and get the data owner’s written approval.

7.5Schema archaeology on undocumented systems

In one line: reflect the schema, profile every column (nulls, distinct values, formats), find the real join keys, and write the data dictionary nobody else did.

Analogy. An archaeologist brushing dust off bones: you do not know what animal it is yet, but measurements and patterns tell you.

from sqlalchemy import create_engine, inspect, text
import pandas as pd

engine = create_engine("postgresql+psycopg://reader:***@replica.internal/crm")
insp = inspect(engine)

for table in insp.get_table_names(schema="public"):
    cols = insp.get_columns(table, schema="public")
    fks = insp.get_foreign_keys(table, schema="public")
    print(table, [c["name"] for c in cols], "FKs:", [(f["constrained_columns"], f["referred_table"]) for f in fks])

def profile(table: str, sample=50_000) -> pd.DataFrame:
    df = pd.read_sql(text(f'SELECT * FROM "{table}" TABLESAMPLE SYSTEM (5) LIMIT {sample}'), engine)
    return pd.DataFrame({
        "null_pct": df.isna().mean().round(3),
        "distinct": df.nunique(),
        "example": df.apply(lambda s: s.dropna().astype(str).head(3).tolist()),
    })

print(profile("tickets"))

Clues to look for. *_id columns without foreign keys (implicit joins), status codes stored as integers (find the lookup table or ask), deleted_at soft deletes, text columns secretly holding JSON, and timestamps in mixed time zones.

Reading the code. TABLESAMPLE profiles a sample so you never scan a huge table. Null percentage and distinct counts quickly reveal keys (high distinct), flags (distinct of 2) and dead columns (mostly null).

7.6PII identification and masking at extraction

In one line: detect and mask personal data before it leaves the source boundary, in the extraction job, so it never lands in your vector store, logs, or prompts.

Analogy. Redacting a document with a black marker before photocopying it, not after it is already on everyone’s desk.

import re
from presidio_analyzer import AnalyzerEngine, PatternRecognizer, Pattern
from presidio_anonymizer import AnonymizerEngine
from presidio_anonymizer.entities import OperatorConfig

pan = PatternRecognizer(supported_entity="IN_PAN",
                        patterns=[Pattern("pan", r"\b[A-Z]{5}[0-9]{4}[A-Z]\b", 0.85)])
aadhaar = PatternRecognizer(supported_entity="IN_AADHAAR",
                            patterns=[Pattern("aadhaar", r"\b\d{4}\s?\d{4}\s?\d{4}\b", 0.6)])

analyzer = AnalyzerEngine()
analyzer.registry.add_recognizer(pan)
analyzer.registry.add_recognizer(aadhaar)
anonymizer = AnonymizerEngine()

text = "Customer Rahul Verma, PAN ABCDE1234F, Aadhaar 1234 5678 9012, called about refund."
results = analyzer.analyze(text=text, language="en")
masked = anonymizer.anonymize(text=text, analyzer_results=results, operators={
    "DEFAULT": OperatorConfig("replace", {"new_value": "<REDACTED>"}),
    "PERSON": OperatorConfig("replace", {"new_value": "<CUSTOMER>"}),
})
print(masked.text)

flowchart LR
  SRC[("Client DB")] --> EXT["Extraction job inside client network"]
  EXT --> DET["Detect PII: Presidio + custom rules"]
  DET --> MASK["Mask or tokenise"]
  MASK --> OUT["Clean data to RAG pipeline"]
  DET --> MAP[("Token vault, client-owned, if re-identification is needed")]

Reading the code. Custom recognisers add Indian ID formats the defaults miss. Operators choose how to mask: replace with a label, or tokenise when someone must later re-identify a record (the token vault stays client-owned).

Trap. Regexes alone miss names and addresses; NER alone misses IDs. Combine both, then measure recall on a hand-labelled sample.

7.7VPC-only, private endpoints, air-gapped inference

In one line: keep all traffic on private networks: private endpoints to cloud model services, or models hosted inside the client’s VPC, and fully offline (air-gapped) when the data cannot touch the internet at all.

Analogy. Three levels of a secure building: (1) visitors use a private corridor (private endpoint), (2) the expert works inside your office (self-hosted in VPC), (3) the room has no windows or doors to the outside at all (air-gapped).

7.8Enterprise auth — SSO, SAML, OIDC, service principals, RBAC

In one line: humans log in through the client’s identity provider (SAML or OIDC); workloads use service principals or IAM roles; RBAC decides what each can see, including which documents RAG may retrieve.

Analogy. The office building’s badge (SSO) opens the front door; your role decides which floors the badge works on (RBAC); the cleaning robot has its own badge (service principal).

  participant App
  participant IdP as Okta or Entra ID

  App->>IdP: redirect, OIDC authorization code flow
  IdP->>U: login + MFA
  IdP-->>App: code
  App->>IdP: exchange code for ID token
  IdP-->>App: ID token with groups claim

Code

import jwt                                  # pip install pyjwt[crypto]
from jwt import PyJWKClient

JWKS = PyJWKClient("https://login.example.com/.well-known/jwks.json")

def verify(token: str) -> dict:
    key = JWKS.get_signing_key_from_jwt(token).key
    return jwt.decode(token, key, algorithms=["RS256"], audience="policy-assistant",
                      issuer="https://login.example.com/")

ROLE_FILTERS = {"support_agent": {"visibility": {"$in": ["public", "internal"]}},
                "compliance": {}}           # no filter: sees everything

def retrieval_filter(claims: dict) -> dict:
    roles = claims.get("groups", [])
    return ROLE_FILTERS["compliance"] if "compliance" in roles else ROLE_FILTERS["support_agent"]
SAML OIDC
Format XML assertions JSON / JWT tokens
Built on Its own spec OAuth 2.0
Common in Older enterprise web apps Modern web, mobile, APIs

Reading the code. verify checks the signature, audience and issuer, so a token meant for another app is rejected. The groups claim becomes a metadata filter that is sent with the retrieval query.

Trap. Filtering documents after the LLM has seen them. Access control must be applied in the retrieval query, or the model can leak restricted text.

7.9Why the security team rejects your first architecture

In one line: they reject what they cannot verify (data leaving the boundary, unclear retention, broad permissions, no audit trail, unvetted third parties), so pre-answer those questions in your design document.

Analogy. Airport security does not hate you; they just need to see everything in a clear plastic bag.

They ask Bring
Where does our data go? Data-flow diagram with every hop, region, and vendor
Does the vendor train on our data? Vendor terms + zero-retention / no-training settings
Who can access what? RBAC matrix, SSO, least-privilege IAM policies
How long are prompts kept? Retention policy + deletion mechanism
Can you prove what happened? Audit logs: who asked what, which docs, which model version
What about prompt injection? Threat model, tool arbiter, red-team results
What if the vendor is down? Fallback model, graceful degradation

  U["User in corp network"] -->|SSO, TLS| APP["App in client VPC"]
  APP -->|private endpoint, TLS| LLM["LLM: zero retention"]
  APP --> VDB[("Vector DB, encrypted, same region")]
  APP --> LOG[("Audit log, 1 year, immutable")]
  SRC[("Source systems")] -->|masked extract| VDB

How to use this. Draw the data-flow diagram before the review and annotate every arrow with encryption, region and retention. Treat the security reviewer as a collaborator: ask early what their checklist is.

Trap. Showing up with a vendor-heavy architecture and no data-flow diagram. Draw the diagram first; it is usually the first thing they ask for.

7.10Inference serving and throughput under real SLAs

In one line: throughput depends on batching, KV-cache memory, and output length; size GPUs from expected concurrent requests and tokens per second, then load-test against the SLA.

Analogy. A bus is more efficient than taxis (batching), but passengers wait for it to fill. Continuous batching is a bus that picks people up at every stop without stopping the route.

Capacity back-of-envelope
peak requests per second (RPS)  = 20
average output tokens           = 250
tokens per second needed        = 20 × 250 = 5,000 tok/s
one GPU serving an 8B model     ≈ 1,500–3,000 tok/s aggregate (measure yours!)
→ 2–4 GPUs + 1 spare for failover

# vLLM: OpenAI-compatible server with continuous batching and paged KV cache
pip install vllm
vllm serve meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct --max-model-len 8192 --gpu-memory-utilization 0.9
Knob Effect
max_tokens Shorter outputs = more requests per GPU
Context length Longer contexts eat KV-cache memory, fewer concurrent requests
Quantisation (AWQ, FP8) More throughput, small quality cost
Batch / concurrency limits Higher throughput vs higher per-request latency

Reading the numbers. The KV cache stores attention state for every active request; paged attention (vLLM) manages it like virtual memory, so more requests fit. Always add a spare GPU for failover, and scale on queue depth and time-to-first-token rather than CPU.

7.11Vector search at 10M+ documents

In one line: at tens of millions of vectors, use approximate indexes (HNSW, IVF-PQ), shard by tenant or time, filter on metadata efficiently, and budget memory explicitly.

Analogy. Finding a friend in a stadium. Checking every seat (flat search) works for a classroom, not 80,000 people. You use sections, rows, and a good guess (approximate search).

Memory estimate (float32)
10,000,000 vectors × 1,536 dims × 4 bytes ≈ 61 GB  (before index overhead)
Options: smaller dims (512), int8 or binary quantisation, product quantisation (PQ)

import faiss, numpy as np

d, nlist, m = 768, 4096, 64                    # dims, clusters, PQ sub-vectors
quantizer = faiss.IndexFlatIP(d)
index = faiss.IndexIVFPQ(quantizer, d, nlist, m, 8)
train = np.random.rand(200_000, d).astype("float32")
index.train(train)
index.add(train)
index.nprobe = 32                              # recall vs speed knob
D, I = index.search(train[:5], 10)

hnsw = faiss.IndexHNSWFlat(d, 32)              # M=32 links per node
hnsw.hnsw.efSearch = 64                        # higher = better recall, slower

flowchart TB
  Q["Query + tenant filter"] --> R["Router by tenant or date"]
  R --> S1["Shard 1: HNSW"]
  R --> S2["Shard 2: HNSW"]
  R --> S3["Shard 3: HNSW"]
  S1 --> M["Merge top-k"]
  S2 --> M
  S3 --> M
  M --> RR["Rerank top 50"]

Reading the code. IVF-PQ clusters vectors (nlist) and compresses them (m sub-vectors) to save memory; nprobe is how many clusters to search, the main recall-versus-speed dial. HNSW’s efSearch plays the same role. Always measure recall against an exact search on a sample.

Trap. Restrictive metadata filters can wreck HNSW recall (most neighbours get filtered out). Check that your database supports filtered search well, or partition by the filter key.

7.12Model versioning, staged rollout, fleet-wide observability

In one line: every response is tagged with model + prompt + index version; new versions go to a small canary slice first; compare quality, latency, and cost before widening.

Analogy. A new recipe goes on the menu at one branch for a week before the whole chain adopts it.

flowchart LR
  V2["New version: prompt v4 + model B"] --> SH["Shadow: run silently, compare offline"]
  SH --> C5["Canary: 5 percent of users"]
  C5 --> C25["25 percent"]
  C25 --> ALL["100 percent"]
  C5 -->|quality or cost worse| RB["Roll back to v3"]
  C25 -->|regression| RB
import hashlib

RELEASES = {"stable": {"prompt": "answer_v3", "model": "gpt-4o-mini", "index": "2026-10-28"},
            "canary": {"prompt": "answer_v4", "model": "gpt-4.1-mini", "index": "2026-10-31"}}
CANARY_PCT = 5

def pick_release(user_id: str) -> dict:
    bucket = int(hashlib.sha256(user_id.encode()).hexdigest(), 16) % 100   # sticky per user
    name = "canary" if bucket < CANARY_PCT else "stable"
    return {"release": name, **RELEASES[name]}

release = pick_release("agent-1042")
# log release fields with every request and trace, then compare dashboards by release

Reading the code. Hashing the user id makes assignment sticky: the same person always lands in the same group, so their experience is consistent and your comparison is fair. Shadow mode runs the new version silently on real traffic without showing users its answers.

Trap. Comparing canary vs stable on different traffic mixes. Use sticky hashing and segment metrics by release on comparable cohorts.

Read: Martin Fowler: canary release · Google SRE: canarying releases · MLflow model registry

7.13Cost modelling and the token-economics talk with a CTO

In one line: translate tokens into business units (cost per ticket, per agent per month, per resolved query), show the levers, and compare against the cost of the problem you are solving.

Analogy. The CTO does not care about litres of fuel; they care about cost per delivery and whether a bigger truck pays off.

def cost_per_query(tok_in=2500, tok_out=300, price_in=0.40, price_out=1.60,
                   cache_hit=0.6, cache_discount=0.9, rerank=0.0002, embed=0.00002):
    effective_in = tok_in * (1 - cache_hit * cache_discount)
    llm = (effective_in * price_in + tok_out * price_out) / 1e6
    return llm + rerank + embed

q = cost_per_query()
queries_per_agent_day, agents, workdays = 60, 120, 22
monthly = q * queries_per_agent_day * agents * workdays
saved_minutes = 4.5 * queries_per_agent_day * agents * workdays
print(f"${q:.4f}/query  ${monthly:,.0f}/month  {saved_minutes / 60:,.0f} agent-hours saved/month")
Lever Typical saving
Smaller model for easy queries (routing) 50–90% on routed traffic
Prompt caching of system + docs Large on repeated prefixes
Trim retrieved context (rerank top 5, not 20) 30–60% input tokens
Cap output length Direct, output tokens cost more
Cache answers to frequent questions Near 100% on hits

Reading the code. Prices are per million tokens, so divide by 1e6. Caching reduces effective input tokens. The last lines convert the bill into agent-hours saved, which is the sentence the CTO actually repeats upstairs.

Trap. Presenting only the LLM bill. Include vector DB, GPUs, observability tools, and engineering time, and the value side (hours saved, errors avoided).

7.14India’s DPDP Act, GDPR, data residency

In one line: process personal data only for a stated purpose with a lawful basis (often consent), collect the minimum, keep it secure, honour user rights, and respect where data must be stored and processed.

Analogy. Borrowing someone’s car: ask first (consent), use it only for the trip you said (purpose limitation), do not copy the keys (minimisation), and return it when asked (erasure).

Concept DPDP Act 2023 (India) GDPR (EU)
Who Data fiduciary / data principal Controller / data subject
Lawful basis Consent or certain “legitimate uses” Six lawful bases
Rights Access, correction, erasure, grievance Access, rectification, erasure, portability, objection
Cross-border Allowed except to countries the government restricts Adequacy, SCCs, and similar safeguards
Penalties Up to ₹250 crore per breach category Up to €20M or 4% of global turnover

  P --> S["Store: encrypted, in approved region"]
  S --> R["Retain only as long as needed"]

Engineering checklist. Purpose recorded per dataset; PII masked before LLM calls where possible; region-pinned storage and model endpoints; deletion that also covers vector stores, caches, logs and backups; a DPIA (impact assessment) for high-risk uses.

Trap. Deleting a record from the main database but leaving its embedding, cache entry and log line behind. Erasure must reach every copy.

Not legal advice; involve the client’s DPO and counsel.

7.15Audit logging, model cards, retention policy

In one line: record who asked what, which documents and model version produced the answer, and what action followed; document each model’s intended use and limits in a model card; delete on schedule.

Analogy. A ship’s logbook: every course change, who ordered it, and why. Model cards are the ship’s specification plate.

import hashlib, json, time

def audit_event(user, question, doc_ids, release, answer, action=None):
    return {
        "ts": time.time(),
        "user_id": user["id"],
        "roles": user["roles"],
        "question_sha256": hashlib.sha256(question.encode()).hexdigest(),   # hash if content is sensitive
        "doc_ids": doc_ids,
        "release": release,                    # prompt/model/index versions
        "answer_chars": len(answer),
        "action": action,                      # e.g. "refund_approved_by:lead-7"
    }

def risk_tier(action: str, amount: int) -> str:
    if action in {"close_account", "deny_claim"}:
        return "high"
    if amount < 2_000:
        return "low"
    return "medium" if amount < 50_000 else "high"

Reviewer UI. Design for speed: show the proposed action, the evidence (retrieved docs, tool outputs), the model’s reasoning summary, and one-click approve / edit / reject with a reason. Feed rejections back into the eval set.

AI-specific runbook items.

  • Kill switch: a feature flag that routes to “a human will help you” instantly.
  • Rollback: prompt, model and index versions pinned in config, not hard-coded.

7.18Presenting trade-offs to a hostile stakeholder panel

In one line: lead with the decision and the number, show the options you rejected and why, name the risks before they do, and say “I don’t know, I’ll find out by Thursday” when true.

Analogy. A courtroom: you win with evidence and a clear argument, not volume.

Structure (10 minutes).

  1. Decision in one sentence.
  2. Why it matters: the business metric.
  3. Options considered (table) with cost, quality, risk.
  4. Recommendation and the evidence (eval results).
  5. Risks + mitigations: say them first.
  6. Ask: what you need from them, by when.
Option Accuracy p95 Monthly cost Data leaves VPC Verdict
A. Public API, large model 93% 2.1 s $9.8k Yes Rejected by security
B. Private endpoint, mid model 91% 1.4 s $4.1k Privately Recommended
C. Self-hosted 8B fine-tuned 88% 0.9 s $6.5k + ops No Phase 2 option

Handling hostile questions. Acknowledge (“That is a fair concern”), answer with evidence or a commitment, and bridge back to the decision. Never argue about feelings; argue about numbers.

Trap. Defending the option you built instead of the option that fits their constraints. In the table, option A has the best accuracy and still loses.

7.19Defending your design to the client’s architects

In one line: architects probe failure modes, scaling limits, coupling, and operability; come with diagrams at two zoom levels, explicit non-functional requirements, and the ADRs behind each choice.

Analogy. A structural engineer reviewing your building: they do not care how pretty the lobby is; they ask what happens in an earthquake.

Questions to pre-answer.

  • What happens when the LLM provider, vector DB, or IdP is down?
  • How does it scale 10×? Where is the first bottleneck?
  • How do you deploy, roll back, and monitor it, and who is on call?
  • How is tenant data isolated? How is access enforced in retrieval?
  • What are the data flows and where is each piece stored and for how long?
  • What is coupled to a single vendor, and how hard is it to swap?

Boss level — The Retrieval Bake-Off

Boss level. “The Discovery Engagement” + “The Data Excavation”: role-play a discovery call, write the PRD-lite, SOW, and three ADRs, then reverse-engineer a messy legacy database with CDC and RapidFuzz entity resolution into a clean sync pipeline.

Suggested steps.

  1. Run a 30-minute discovery call using the question bank; write the problem statement with a baseline and target (7.1).
  2. Draw the stakeholder map and pick the first slice on the effort/value chart (7.2).
  3. Write the PRD-lite, the SOW with assumptions and a change process, and three ADRs (7.3).
  4. Profile the legacy schema, verify joins with match-rate queries, and write the data dictionary (7.5).
  5. Stream changes with CDC from a replica, masking PII at extraction (7.4, 7.6).
  6. Use RapidFuzz to resolve duplicate customers, and report precision and recall on a hand-labelled sample.
  7. Present the final options table to a mock hostile panel (7.18).

Appendix B — Debugging checklist for a failing RAG answer

Work down the list in order; most problems are in the first four items.

  1. Can you state the problem as a metric? If not, go back to discovery (7.1).
  2. Is the scope one slice a sponsor can see in weeks? Shrink it using the effort/value chart (7.2).
  3. Are assumptions written in the SOW? Late data access is only your risk if it is undocumented (7.3).
  4. Is anything touching production directly? Move to a replica or CDC (7.4).
  5. Do your joins have match rates? Verify them; never trust column names (7.5).
  6. Is PII masked before it leaves the source? Check recall on a labelled sample (7.6).
  7. Can security see every data flow? Bring the annotated data-flow diagram (7.7, 7.9).
  8. Is access control in the retrieval query? Never filter after generation (7.8).
  9. Do you have a cost-per-query number and value? Show the levers and agent-hours saved (7.13).
  10. Does deletion reach vectors, caches and logs? Test an erasure request end to end (7.14).
  11. Is there a tested kill switch and rollback? Practise before launch (7.12, 7.17).

Appendix C — Final self-assessment

Answer without looking. Anything you cannot explain in two sentences is a chapter to revisit.

1. Why ask about the past, not hypotheticals?

Past behaviour is evidence; hypotheticals get polite yeses. (7.1)

2. Who are gatekeepers, and when should you meet them?

Security, legal and data owners who can block; meet them early. (7.2)

3. What does an ADR record?

Why one option was chosen over alternatives, with consequences. (7.3)

4. Why use a replica and CDC?

To avoid loading production and to stream changes as events. (7.4)

5. How do you verify an undocumented join?

Run match-rate queries rather than trusting names. (7.5)

6. Where should PII be masked?

At extraction, before it leaves the source boundary. (7.6)

7. Name the four network patterns.

Public API, private endpoint, self-hosted in VPC, air-gapped. (7.7)

8. Why apply RBAC in the retrieval query?

Otherwise the model may see and leak restricted text. (7.8)

9. What is the first artefact security asks for?

A data-flow diagram. (7.9)

10. How do you estimate GPUs needed?

Peak RPS times output tokens, divided by measured tokens per second, plus a spare. (7.10)

11. What hurts HNSW recall?

Very restrictive metadata filters. (7.11)

12. Why use sticky hashing in a canary?

The same user always gets the same version, so comparisons are fair. (7.12)

13. What should a cost talk include besides the LLM bill?

Vector DB, GPUs, tooling, engineering time and the value saved. (7.13)

14. Must erasure cover embeddings?

Yes: vectors, caches, logs and backups. (7.14)

15. Why store a hash of the question in audit logs?

To prove what was asked without keeping sensitive text. (7.15)

16. How do you spot a rubber-stamp review?

Near-100% approval in seconds; track time and disagreement. (7.16)

17. Name two AI-specific incident steps.

Roll back prompts or models, and check traces for data exposure. (7.17)

18. Why can the most accurate option lose?

It may break constraints such as data leaving the VPC. (7.18)

19. What are the two C4 levels to present first?

Context, then containers. (7.19)
